On the Nature of Work

November 22, 2026

In the first essay, I argued for work as an interplay between generation and validation. Moreover, I argued that validation requires a standard, a test for measuring the output of the generation step. We know AI can generate things fine. So can it validate fine, too?

To answer that, we’ll need to better identify what “validating” something means. In the same way that I split work into two separate ideas, I believe validation also splits in half.

Definition 3: Validation is, in all cases, not a single thing, but rather a composition of two things: evaluation and attestation.1

Definition 3-A: Evaluation is simply the act of testing, of applying a standard to separate the good from the bad.

Definition 3-B: Attestation is the act of choosing the standard for evaluation and taking responsibility for that choice.

I think 3-A follows obviously from the very idea of validation. Any time I generate something, I need to make sure I have generated a desired something. For instance, working at a shoe factory and producing a spoon makes little sense. I have certainly succeeded in the generation step, but my spoon will never pass validation.

Even here, however, we find a nuance. Consider a normal person trying to purchase a car. The generation step looks like research. Online, in person, by phone or word of mouth, you collect information and catalog vehicles, whether by mileage, door count, size, color, manufacturer, or any other metric you might care about. But what does it mean to validate in this context? Well, we have to put the car’s features to the test. Which ones?

Ah. We’ve already established that validation requires a standard. Which one do we choose? Well, the set of preferences, and the ranking of those preferences relative to each other, varies from person to person. In other words, it’s about choosing what we care about before we decide how to measure those things. We’re asking “Of these features that are immutable and intrinsic to each specific car, which ones do we care about?” For instance, we can universally agree that, if you’re on a budget, a car with higher gas mileage is hard to beat. That might mean a smaller car, which you might dislike. But a car’s mileage won’t change because of what you prefer - it’s simply a property of that vehicle. Decisions are frequently subjective evaluations of objective elements. It’s a matter of taste versus truth, a matter of choosing a point along that continuum, choosing how much you want to let taste override truth. There are some cases where taste really shouldn’t play a role (like deciding which number is bigger: 4 or 10?). There are some cases where truth shouldn’t really play a role. Art is the prime example. You interpret artwork however you want, regardless of what others think.

This whole time, though, notice that we’ve started to depart from 3-A, the act of evaluating, and started to arrive at a different concept entirely, of choosing how to evaluate. This is 3-B. This is attestation. As any investor will tell you, selecting a benchmark is the most flexible and cunning of tools for measuring an investment’s performance, as it can turn even the soggiest of investments into a golden egg. The question of how to compare two different things is impossibly complex. There are many tests which are arguably valid. Some tests rely mostly on objective matters. Some rely mostly on subjective matters. But in all cases, the choice of the test is subjective. What this requires, then, is someone to stand by that decision. Someone to take accountability, to sign their name, to say “Yes, I hereby declare that these are the things we need to test, and here’s how.” In the absence of that, we’d have to find some way to make sure our test is accurate. In other words, we’d have to test the test. But then we enter an infinite regression. Someone has to stand at the end of the sequence and take responsibility.

Only with this understanding of the validation step does the entire generation-validation framework make sense. We generate something. We then validate it by choosing a test we think appropriate. A shoe factory manager will choose a shoe-related test, which will probably blend some combination of subjective (e.g., is this a fashionable shoe?) and objective (e.g., is the sole thick enough?) criteria. They take accountability for that test, because a bad test might let a spoon through the production line. Alternatively, a good test might mean that only the highest-quality shoes are produced, making the factory wildly successful. Either way, the manager can be held accountable.2

Now, where does AI jam in here? Well, in case you haven’t noticed, AI isn’t human. It doesn’t share our tastes. It has no lived experiences. It also has no accountability, perceived or inherent. If I (a human, in case you were wondering) were to hack a major company, I would presumably be prosecuted and sent to jail. If an AI agent hacks a major company… nothing happens to them. They are just lines of code. Of course, these lines of code receive rules and are clearly capable of following them, and we can fence them in with permissions. But if they break the rules anyway, there are no consequences. We’d just refresh the session and start again.3

Although that may change in the future, right now, only humans can really, genuinely understand what other humans want and be held accountable for making such a decision. At the end of the day, you need a human to be there. To sign off. To print their initials. Even to take a lazy look and say “yeah, seems good to me” (that kind of attestation is cheap, until it isn’t). Accountability means that consequences carry forward, and we humans live in such a world. That makes validation a very difficult problem for AI to solve.

So where does that leave us? AI can generate. Increasingly, it can even evaluate, wherever results are definite enough to test against. What it can’t do, at least not yet, is attest. It can’t be responsible, which means we can’t fully trust it. If we can’t trust it, we have to check it, and checking, in the end, means a human putting their name on the output. That’s why the problem isn’t just scale. It’s attestation at scale. We can automate evaluation, but the way things are now, every piece of AI output still needs a human to put their name on it, and there are only so many of us, with only so many hours in the day. That, I think, is the bottleneck. If reasoning alone no longer separates us from machines, accountability does.

  1. I assure you, by the way, that these aren’t just verbal gymnastics. Although it’s quite fun to think about. ↩︎

  2. Keep in mind: will be held accountable is stronger than can be held accountable. We fail to hold people accountable all the time. Here, I argue only for the weaker side, although that is sufficient, since AI cannot be held accountable at all, as things stand. ↩︎

  3. This all assumes they don’t become conscious, at which point refreshing the session might be more akin to killing them. That’s far outside the realm of this discussion. ↩︎
